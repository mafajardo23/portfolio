The Blob Tree

A concept mobile app for tracking mood and emotional wellbeing, grounded in a full needfinding study and tested through paper prototypes. The core idea, The Blob Tree, lets people log more than one emotion a day using customizable characters instead of forcing their day into a single mood.

Role

UX Researcher & Designer, solo project

Timeline

Fall 2025

Team

Solo, Human-Computer Interaction coursework project

Tools

Zoom interviews, empathy mapping, paper prototyping

Overview

This project started from a simple observation: most mood tracking apps ask people to sum up an entire day in one emoji. That felt off to me, especially after watching Inside Out 2, which spends the whole movie showing how many emotions a person can hold at once. I wanted to find out whether people actually wanted a tool that captured that complexity, and if so, what would make them actually use it. I ran a full needfinding study before designing anything, then built and tested paper prototypes based on what I learned.

Problem

Three interviews across a deliberately wide range of relationships to journaling surfaced a pattern I hadn't expected. I talked to a therapist who recommends journaling to her clients but rarely does it herself, a pre-med student who avoids it entirely, and a student who values it but can't stay consistent. Almost nobody doubted that tracking their mood would help them. The gap was between knowing that and actually doing it. A few specific frictions kept coming up:

  • Reflection felt like one more task on an already full day, especially for people already prone to perfectionism
  • Predetermined mood options, one emoji, one word, didn't match how people actually felt, which made the tool feel disconnected from them instead of clarifying anything
  • Writing felt like it had to be done "right," which stopped people before they started
  • People process emotions socially as much as privately, through friends, metaphor, or media, and most tracking tools only account for the private, written version of that

One of the therapists I interviewed put it simply: "I usually just keep it in my head." Coming from someone whose job is to encourage exactly this habit in other people, that gap felt like the real problem to design around.

Process

I recruited three interview participants deliberately spread across the spectrum of belief in the practice: a professional who studies emotional patterns for a living, someone who gets stressed just thinking about journaling, and someone who values it but struggles to stay consistent. I used the Institute of Design's Empathy Field Guide as an interview framework and built an empathy map from each conversation.

  • Synthesized the interviews into POV statements and How Might We questions, so the direction stayed grounded in real behavior instead of assumptions
  • Looked outside the mood tracking category for inspiration: studied how Strava uses social accountability, kudos and visible streaks, to keep people working out, and how Duolingo turns habit formation into something closer to a game than a chore
  • Landed on The Blob Tree, a mood logging tool originally created by psychologist Pip Wilson and illustrator Ian Long, and adapted it into the app's central mechanic: colorless, genderless "Blob" characters that users can log, personalize, and revisit, so a single day can hold more than one emotion
  • Sketched five variations each of the three core screens (Home, the Blob Tree, and Calendar view), working through navigation placement and how much of the interface should rely on visuals versus text
  • Tested paper prototypes with three separate users and gathered structured feedback on the concept, the navigation, and the specific features they wished it had

Full write-ups of both stages are on Medium: Needfinding: Tracking Mood and Emotional Wellbeing. Prototyping: Tracking Mood and Emotional Wellbeing.

Solution

The final concept splits the experience across three tabs. Home stays deliberately light, a daily insight plus quick access to journaling, breathing, or a chat feature, since someone opening the app in a stressful moment shouldn't have to think hard to find help. The Blob Tree is where people log their actual emotions for the day, using customizable Blobs instead of a single mood score, so the tool doesn't force one feeling onto a day that held several. Calendar view uses color and Blob density instead of one color per day, so looking back on a week or month still reflects that complexity instead of flattening it. A lightweight social layer lets friends see each other's trees moving without exposing the specifics behind them, since testers wanted connection without giving up privacy.

Outcomes

Usability testing on the paper prototypes confirmed the core idea: all three testers responded to the Blob Tree concept and said it made tracking feel more like something fun than a chore. Testing also surfaced clear next steps I hadn't designed for yet, including guided journal prompts, an Apple Watch integration for the breathing feature, and a monthly or weekly "wrapped"-style summary of a person's emotional data. This project is currently at the concept and paper-prototype stage. My plan for the next round of testing is a larger, more varied group of participants completing structured tasks in the app, so I can track completion rates and time on task alongside the qualitative feedback.

Reflection

The biggest lesson from this project was the gap between what people believe and what they actually do. A therapist who recommends journaling to her own clients still doesn't do it herself, mostly because of time. That's a more useful insight than anything a survey question could have given me, and it only came out because I was interviewing for behavior and stories, not opinions. It also made me more confident pulling inspiration from outside the immediate product category. Strava and Duolingo aren't mood trackers, but the reasons people keep opening them told me more about habit formation than any journaling app I looked at.
